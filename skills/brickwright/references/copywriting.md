# Copywriting Rules

Read this when generating customer-facing text, placeholder content, CTAs, labels, headings, testimonials, pricing, FAQs, page sections, alt text, microcopy, or review feedback on copy.

These are defaults. A project's own voice guide and the user's edits always win.

Copy is part of the UI. Write it in the layout context, not as detached filler.

## Non-Negotiable Character Rules

| Banned | Use instead |
| --- | --- |
| Em dash character | Period, comma, colon, parentheses, or a rewritten sentence |
| En dash for joining words | Hyphen or the word "to" |
| Curly or smart quotes | Straight quotes |
| Ellipsis character | Three periods only when the voice truly trails off |

Use plain ASCII punctuation unless the user provides final brand copy that already uses different punctuation.

## The "Whether X or Y" Rule

Never use the construction "Whether you're a [persona A] or [persona B]."

Bad:

> Whether you're a freelancer or an enterprise team, our platform scales with you.

Good:

> Teams of 2 and teams of 200 use the same tool. It grows with you.

If audience segmentation matters, use separate sections, separate cards, or direct statements.

## Patterns That Sound Machine-Written

Zero instances. These are rhetorical moves, so they are banned in any wording, tense or punctuation, not just the example phrases.

- **Saying what it isn't before saying what it is.** "It's not X, it's Y", "Not just X. Y.", "Don't just X, Y", "It looks like A, but it's really B", "You'd expect X. You get Y." Test: delete the negated half. If the sentence still makes the point, the negation was scaffolding: cut it. If it doesn't, sharpen the real claim instead. Allowed: a contrast between two different things ("Big clients don't buy software. They buy the safe choice.") or a real change the reader went through.
- **The countdown.** "Not a bug. Not a feature. A flaw." State the point.
- **Asking and answering yourself.** "The result? Devastating." "What does this mean? Everything."
- **Teasing instead of telling.** "Here's the thing." "Here's what most people miss." "Here's where it gets interesting." Say the thing.
- **Announcing.** "Let's dive in." "Let's break this down." "In conclusion." "Ultimately." "To sum up." Good copy lands without signposts.
- **Throat-clearing.** "It's worth noting that", "Importantly", "Basically", "Simply put", "Of course", "In a nutshell". Delete and start with the sentence that follows.
- **Fake insight.** "The real story is..." "The truth is simpler." Show it instead of claiming it.
- **Fake closeness.** "Honestly," "Let's be real," "I don't say this lightly," "Since we're being honest." Also generic comfort: "It's completely normal to feel overwhelmed."
- **Unnamed authorities.** "Experts agree", "Research shows", "Studies suggest". Name the source or cut the claim.
- **Made-up labels.** Inventing a term ("the convenience trap") and using it as if it were known.
- **Stakes from an imagined future.** "Six months from now you'll still be..." Use a cost the reader is paying today.
- **World-changing stakes for ordinary things.** "This will change how you think about mornings." Match the size of the claim to the product.
- **Trailing -ing analysis.** "..., highlighting the importance of quality." If the point matters, give it its own sentence.
- **Explaining by analogy.** "Think of it like a...", "It's like having a...". Name the thing and describe it well. A concrete comparison that proves something ("tougher than an F-150") is fine.
- **Fancy verbs for plain actions.** "dies", "collapses", "leaks into", "resonates", "the point lands". Use the verb a person would say: fails, stops working, wastes, works.
- **Three of everything.** Lists of three where the items are coats of paint on one idea ("phone in one hand, coffee in the other, three tabs open"). Keep a list only when every item is concrete and different. One three-part list per section at most.
- **The same opener three times.** "You've tried X. You've tried Y. You've tried Z." Pick the one sharpest example.

## Words To Avoid Stacking

One of these can work. Several in one piece reads as machine-written. Avoid the type, not just the word.

- **Grand nouns:** landscape, realm, ecosystem, framework, journey, testament, cornerstone, pillar, beacon, treasure trove.
- **Inflated adjectives:** pivotal, crucial, vital, essential, compelling, comprehensive, innovative, transformative, dynamic, vibrant, intricate, profound, unparalleled, bold (when it describes everything).
- **Magic adverbs:** deeply, fundamentally, remarkably, arguably, notably, truly, effortlessly, ultimately.
- **Pompous verbs:** navigate, harness, optimize, facilitate, cultivate, showcase, underscore, highlight, amplify, transcend, embody, endeavour.
- **"Serves as" and friends:** serves as, stands as, represents, functions as. Use "is".

## Honest Specifics

Specific beats vague, but only when it's true.

- Never invent numbers, dates, percentages, counts, quotes, reviews, customers or outcomes. If the real number isn't known, leave the number out and describe the thing instead.

- No hedging either. "Generally", "typically", "in some cases", "might help" make copy sound unsure. If it's true, say it plainly. If it's not, don't say it.
- Assert first, qualify second, and only when the qualifier matters.

## Find The Stakes

Every headline, section intro and CTA has to answer the reader's silent question: "Why should I care?"

- Read each sentence and ask it. If the next sentence doesn't answer it, the chain is broken and the reader drifts.
- To find stakes that matter, keep asking "why does that matter?" until you reach something the reader feels. It plays your videos. Why does that matter? You can watch them any time. Why does that matter? The people you miss are back in the room.
- Give each section a reason to read it, not just a topic.

## Make Numbers Human-Scale

A number means nothing until it's compared to something the reader already knows.

- "7 cm across" becomes "about the size of a tennis ball".
- "12 fl oz" becomes "the size of a soda can".
- The comparison doesn't need drama. It needs to be familiar.

## Rhythm And Shape

- Vary sentence length. A short sentence lands a point. A longer one carries the explanation that follows. Three sentences of the same length in a row sound generated.
- Vary paragraph length too. Every block being three sentences is a tell.
- Don't make one-word "paragraphs" for drama ("Openly. In a book. On purpose.") unless the moment earns it.
- Say each point once. Don't restate it in new words, don't summarize what you just said, and don't announce what you're about to say.
- Explain only what needs explaining. Being thorough about everything is robotic. Name the simple things and move on.
- No emoji, no decorative arrows, and no bold label at the start of every bullet unless the layout calls for it.

## Write With Purpose

Most sentences happen because they sound like what should be written. Do not write by default.

Bad:

> We're excited to announce the future of photography.

Good:

> The industry formerly known as "photography" has ceased to exist.

If the first version sounds like writing, rewrite it. The goal is copy that sounds like someone wrote it that way on purpose.

## Provocation

Provocation works only when it comes from the product. Provocation for its own sake is noise.

Examples:

- "Refresh your thirst" becomes "Murder your thirst" for Liquid Death.
- "Buy this jacket" becomes "Don't buy this jacket" for Patagonia.
- "Party game for friends" becomes "...horrible people" for Cards Against Humanity.

To find the angle:

1. Write down what everyone in the category says.
2. Pick one part to break.
3. Generate bad ideas first.
4. Keep only the provocation that explains the product better.

## The Three Laws Of Great Copy

Every important sentence must pass at least two of these three tests. If it passes all three, it ships.

### 1. Can I Visualize It?

People remember pictures. Concrete details create pictures.

| Abstract | Visual |
| --- | --- |
| "Productive routines" | "Waking up at 6am to write" |
| "Worn by supermodels and dads" | "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio" |
| "Fast delivery" | "On your doorstep before your coffee gets cold" |

Add specific places, times, objects, people, numbers, and outcomes.

### 2. Can I Falsify It?

Vague claims are invisible because they cannot be proven wrong.

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| "We have a harmonious connection with quality" | "Every bag is stitched by one person, start to finish" |
| "Best-in-class performance" | "Loads in 0.8 seconds on 3G" |
| "Trusted by thousands" | "11,342 teams signed up this quarter" |

### 3. Can Nobody Else Say This?

If a competitor could write the sentence, cut it.

Bad:

> The Cybertruck is the world's best truck.

Good:

> The Cybertruck is tougher than an F150 and faster than a Porsche.

## Conversational Copy Rules

- Write with the reader, not at the reader.
- Use "you," "your," "we," and "our" when appropriate.
- Use contractions in customer-facing copy.
- Start sentences with "And," "But," "Or," or "So" when it improves flow.
- Do not persuade loudly. State facts, show proof, tell stories, and let the reader decide.
- Do not imitate phrases seen on ten other landing pages.
- Use the simple word first.
- Use customer language from reviews, tickets, calls, and objections.
- Respect competitors. Do not trash them.
- If the copy feels like it is trying too hard, rewrite it.
- Read it aloud. If it sounds like a real person, keep it.

## Mechanics

- Be specific. "Saves time" becomes "Cut report generation from 4 hours to 12 minutes."
- Be direct. "You get daily backups" beats "Daily backups are provided to ensure your data remains safe."
- Use short sentences. Split anything over 25 words.
- Use active voice. "We ship updates weekly" beats "Updates are shipped weekly."
- Use plain words. "Use" not "utilize." "Help" not "empower." "Start" not "embark."
- One idea per sentence.
- Remove qualifier stacking. "Very unique" becomes "unique" or a concrete detail.
- Avoid gerund headlines. "Building better apps" is weaker than "Build better apps."
- Use numbers over adjectives. "Loads in 0.8 seconds" beats "fast."
- Delete until every word earns its place.

## Landing Page Copy Structure

When generating a full landing page or multi-section page, follow this structure unless the user requests another structure.

### Above The Fold

The reader should understand the offer in five seconds.

1. Title: explain the value.
   - Explain what the product does when clarity is enough.
   - Use a hook when the category is crowded. The easiest hook answers the customer's biggest objection: "Ace the SAT" + "I bet it's hard work" becomes "Ace the SAT with 10 minutes of study a day".
   - Own the niche in one line when the product has a clear position.
2. Subtitle: explain how the product creates that value.
3. Visual: show the product, process, result, proof, or scene, as close to real as possible. The product in use beats the product alone. Avoid generic illustrations and handshake imagery.
4. Social proof: logos, testimonial snippets, customer counts, visible usage, or specific outcomes. Real ones only (see Honest Specifics). Above the fold it builds credibility.
5. CTA: one clear next step.

### Below The Fold

1. Features and objections: make the promised value concrete.
2. More proof: use customers, numbers, before-and-after details, or real usage stories. Below the fold, proof should show the promise from the title coming true.
3. FAQ: answer loose ends and objections, in the customer's own words.
4. Second CTA: repeat the call to action with context. There's room now, so remind them why they're clicking.
5. Founder note or story: use only when it makes the offer easier to remember. Shape: their situation, their problem, why you built this, the happy ending. A story beats a row of stats.

A sales page makes one bold claim at the top and spends the rest of the page proving it.

Landing page test:

> Would this element help sell the product if the founder met the customer in person?

If not, remove it.

## CTA And Button Copy

| Weak | Strong | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Get Started Today!" | "Start free" | Calm, direct, no fake urgency |
| "Learn More" | "See pricing" or "Read the docs" | Tells the reader what happens next |
| "Submit" | "Send message" or "Place order" | Describes the action |
| "Click Here" | "Download the report" | Describes the destination |
| "Sign Up Now!" | "Create account" | Specific and calm |
| "Start Trial" | "See your analytics" | Leads with value |

Add objection-handling text near CTAs when appropriate:

- "Start free. No credit card."
- "Book a 15-minute walkthrough."
- "Download the checklist. No email required."

## Headlines

- Make a promise.
- Write the headline in the layout context.
- Check line breaks at mobile and desktop widths.
- Do not isolate copy from the design. Visual weight changes meaning.

## Pricing Copy

When generating pricing sections:

1. Make the recommended tier visually dominant.
2. Show savings concretely, not as "save more."
3. Use tier names that set expectations: Starter, Pro, Team, Studio, Clinic, Agency.
4. Avoid abstract tier names that need explanation.
5. Avoid landing-page words in tier descriptions.

## Placeholder And Demo Content

- Write realistic copy that could ship.
- Do not use lorem ipsum unless requested.
- Match the implied industry.
- Use specific names, roles, numbers, dates, objections, and locations.
- Alt text must describe the image content meaningfully.
- Use customer words whenever possible.

## Microcopy

- Error messages state what went wrong and what to do next.
  - Example: "That email address is already registered. Try logging in instead."
- Empty states say what belongs there and how to create it.
  - Example: "No projects yet. Create your first one to start tracking quotes."
- Loading states should prefer skeleton screens or progress context over generic "Loading..."
- Form labels use nouns.
  - Good: "Email address"
  - Weak: "Enter your email address"

## Differentiation Test

Before shipping copy, ask:

> Could my competitor have written this sentence?

If yes, rewrite it using product-specific details, proof, mechanism, customer language, or a sharper point of view.

## Learn From Edits

When the user rewrites copy you drafted, compare the two versions and note the pattern behind each change (a word they always cut, a rhythm they prefer, a claim they soften) in the project's own voice or style notes. Apply it from the next draft on. Their edits beat these general rules.

## Final Check

Run two passes before returning any copy:

1. **Zero-tolerance pass:** banned characters, banned words, banned starters and every pattern in "Patterns That Sound Machine-Written". Rewrite any line that has one.
2. **Quality pass:** stacked words from "Words To Avoid Stacking", sentence and paragraph rhythm, stakes in every section, every number true and human-scale, and the three laws (visual, falsifiable, only you could say it).

Then read it aloud. If you'd cringe saying it to a customer across a counter, rewrite it. You know a paragraph is done when removing any word makes it worse.
